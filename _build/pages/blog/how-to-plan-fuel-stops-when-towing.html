How to plan fuel stops when towing a travel trailer or fifth wheel

November 25, 2026 · By David Kulagin, maker of FuelPilot

Work out your real towing MPG, multiply it by your usable tank, keep a quarter tank in reserve, and plan a stop before every stretch where stations are far apart. Then pick stations you can get a trailer in and out of, and check the plan again after every fill-up.

1. Find your real towing MPG

The number on your dash when you're not towing is no use here. A trailer is heavy and it's a wall of air behind your truck. When the Department of Energy's Oak Ridge National Laboratory tested an SUV pulling a 3,500 lb enclosed cargo trailer, fuel economy dropped by about 30% in city driving and by about 50% at 80 mph. A big travel trailer or fifth wheel can do the same or worse.

The way to know your number is the fill-up method:

  1. Fill the tank and reset your trip meter.
  2. Tow as you normally would.
  3. At the next fill-up, divide the miles driven by the gallons it took to fill back up.

Do it over two or three tanks and take the average. Your truck's MPG readout can be off, and one tank into a headwind will make you think it's worse than it is.

2. Work out your range, and keep a reserve

Range is usable tank size times towing MPG. A 36-gallon tank at 9 MPG towing is 324 miles. Don't plan to use all of it. Keep a quarter tank in reserve and you've got about 243 miles between stops.

The reserve is for the things you can't plan: a closed station, a pump that won't take your card, a detour, a long climb, a headwind. The towing range calculator does this math for your rig.

3. Plan around the long empty stretches

In most of the East you'll pass a station every few miles. In parts of the West and in rural Canada you can go well over 100 miles without one. Look at the whole route before you leave and mark the gaps. Fill up before every gap that's longer than your range after reserve, even if the tank isn't low yet. Fuel there may cost more. Running out costs more.

4. Slow down

Air drag climbs fast with speed, and a trailer adds a lot of it. That's why the Oak Ridge test lost so much more at 80 mph than around town. Dropping your cruising speed is the easiest way to stretch your range while towing. Many trailer tires are also rated for lower speeds than car tires, so check the sidewall.

5. Pick stations you can get out of

The cheapest station on the map is no good if you can't swing a 35-foot trailer through it. Before you commit to a stop:

  • Check it on satellite view. Look for pumps you can pull straight through and an exit that doesn't need a tight turn.
  • Prefer the outside pumps, with room to pull forward when you're done.
  • For diesel, many larger stations and travel centers have diesel at the car pumps or separate lanes that fit an RV. Truck lanes are built for semis, so check the station's rules before you pull in.
  • Plan your stops when you'd want a break anyway. A fuel stop is a good time for a walk-around: hitch, safety chains, lights and tire pressures.

6. Check the plan after every fill-up

Things change once you're moving. Wind, hills and traffic change your real MPG, and prices change through the day. Every time you fill up, look at the next stretch again and decide where the next stop is.

Questions

How much does towing a travel trailer reduce gas mileage?

It depends on the trailer's weight and shape and how fast you drive. In a Department of Energy test at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, an SUV towing a 3,500 lb enclosed trailer lost about 30% of its fuel economy in city driving and about 50% at 80 mph. Measure your own with the fill-up method over two or three tanks.

How often should I stop for fuel when towing?

Plan stops from your range after reserve. Multiply your usable tank by your towing MPG, then take off a quarter tank. A 36-gallon tank at 9 MPG gives about 243 miles between stops. Stop earlier before any stretch with no stations.

How much fuel should I keep in reserve when towing?

A quarter tank is a common rule. It covers a closed station, a detour or a headwind. Keep more in remote areas or in winter.

Can I use truck stop diesel lanes with a fifth wheel?

Some stations allow it and some don't. Truck lanes are built for semis, so check the station's rules first. Many larger stations and travel centers have diesel at car pumps or lanes that fit an RV.
